Overview

A meaningful contribution should not end with what one team accomplished during a single competition cycle. We therefore designed our contributions to be useful beyond NariViva, providing new biological parts, reproducible experimental protocols, and transferable Human Practices and Education frameworks that future iGEM teams can adapt to their own projects.

A modular naringenin biosynthesis platform

The biosynthetic pathway developed in NariViva is not limited to naringenin as a final product. Naringenin is a central intermediate in flavonoid biosynthesis and can serve as a branching point toward multiple flavonoid classes. For example, downstream flavone synthases can convert naringenin toward flavones such as apigenin, while isoflavone synthase provides access to isoflavones. Alternatively, F3H channels naringenin into dihydroflavonols, which can subsequently lead to flavonols such as kaempferol, quercetin, and myricetin, or enter the DFR/ANS branch toward anthocyanins.

This branching structure gives our TAL–4CL–CHS–CHI pathway potential value beyond the production of a single target molecule. The upstream biosynthetic module can be retained while downstream enzymes are exchanged according to the desired flavonoid product. Thus, future teams could use the same core pathway as a starting point for constructing different flavonoid-producing systems rather than rebuilding the upstream pathway from scratch.

Two regulatory architectures for different applications

NariViva also developed two related regulatory architectures that illustrate different ways of reusing the same biosynthetic module.

The first architecture connects pH-responsive regulation directly to the naringenin biosynthetic pathway. In this configuration, environmental pH is converted into a change in pathway expression, resulting in a corresponding change in enzyme expression and naringenin production. This provides a relatively simple environment-responsive biosynthetic module that could be connected to other metabolic pathways or downstream products.

The second architecture incorporates a negative-feedback layer into the pH-responsive system. Instead of simply transmitting changes in environmental pH to the final metabolic output, the feedback circuit compensates for changes in pathway activity and can therefore buffer variation in product output. In our characterization, the pH-responsive pathway without feedback showed increasing enzyme expression and naringenin production across the tested pH range, whereas the feedback-regulated architecture produced a substantially more stable output. This demonstrates that the regulatory layer can alter the input–output behavior of the biosynthetic system rather than merely increasing pathway expression.

The two architectures therefore provide different reusable design principles. The pH-responsive pathway can be used when environmental sensing is intended to directly control biosynthetic output, whereas the feedback-regulated architecture can be adapted when output buffering or stabilization is desired. The regulatory layer and biosynthetic layer can also be considered as modular components, allowing future teams to combine environmental sensing, feedback control, and alternative metabolic pathways according to their own design objectives.

Importantly, the current feedback configuration represents a characterized version of the system rather than a universally optimal setting. Our results suggest that the initial feedback strength can compensate for pH-dependent variation across a relatively broad range. Future optimization could tune the feedback strength or regulatory threshold so that lower-pH responsiveness is retained while excessive increases in production at higher pH are prevented. This provides a further engineering direction for adapting the same composite architecture to different applications.

Together, these designs extend the potential reuse of our parts from individual DNA sequences to modular design principles: environmental sensing → regulatory control → core biosynthesis → downstream product diversification. Future iGEM teams can therefore modify one layer while retaining the others, using NariViva as a starting point for constructing new environment-responsive or feedback-regulated biosynthetic systems.

02

Reproducible Experimental Workflows

From experimental records to reusable protocols

One challenge we encountered during NariViva was that characterization of regulatory systems often depends not only on the genetic construct, but also on experimental conditions.

To make our experience more transferable, we organized our experimental procedures into protocols that can be followed by future teams.

Our experimental records currently include:

  • bacterial culture and sample preparation;
  • pH-response characterization;
  • fluorescence and OD measurement;
  • naringenin-response characterization;
  • reporter-system troubleshooting;
  • protein-expression validation;
  • naringenin production validation.

The original experimental records are maintained in our laboratory notebook. We further organized the relevant procedures into reusable protocols so that future teams can reproduce the experiments under comparable conditions.

Example: pH-response characterization

We characterized the PCadBA-based regulatory system across a broad pH range rather than testing only a single environmental condition.

The tested conditions covered:

  • pH 4.0, 4.5, 5.0, 5.5, 6.0, 6.5, and 7.0

For each condition, we monitored both growth and fluorescence. This distinction is important because a change in fluorescence cannot always be interpreted as a regulatory response if the tested condition substantially affects cell growth.

Our results showed an overall increase in relative fluorescence from pH 4.0 to pH 6.5, while the pH 7.0 condition showed an unexpected decrease. The pH 4.0 condition also exhibited reduced growth.

Instead of treating every deviation as evidence for a new mechanism, we documented these observations together with the corresponding growth data and retained the original system for subsequent engineering.

This provides future teams with not only a characterization result, but also an example of how to interpret environmental-response data together with cellular growth.

Example: naringenin-response characterization

We also characterized the FdeR-Pfde system using a naringenin concentration gradient.

The tested concentrations included:

  • 0, 5, 10, 15, 25, 50, 75, and 100 μM

The response of the forward system increased with naringenin concentration and approached a high-response region. When incorporated into the negative-feedback architecture, the output approached a low level at higher naringenin concentrations.

These data provided the experimental basis for incorporating the FdeR-Pfde module into our feedback design.

The protocol and corresponding experimental records can therefore serve as a starting point for teams interested in constructing or characterizing naringenin-responsive genetic circuits.

From VIOCES to BLOOM：Listening, Learning, Growing

Beyond our biological contributions, NariViva also developed a reusable approach for connecting Human Practices with Education. We developed VIOCES and BLOOM as complementary frameworks that connect stakeholder engagement, knowledge development, and educational practice.

Listening is the starting point. Through Human Practices, we listened to different voices, including those of doctors, experts, industry, the public, and other iGEM teams. These perspectives helped us understand different needs and concerns and provided information that could be considered during project development.

Learning is the process of transforming these experiences and scientific knowledge into something that can be understood and shared. The perspectives collected through Human Practices can inform project development, while the scientific knowledge generated through the project can be communicated to people with different levels of scientific background.

Growing means allowing this knowledge to develop beyond its original context. Educational content cannot always be applied to every audience in the same way. By adapting and modifying our educational activities according to different learners, contexts, and feedback, we can make the resources more flexible and reusable.

Together, VIOCES and BLOOM connect these three stages:

Listening → Learning → Growing

VIOCES provides a structured framework for collecting and synthesizing diverse perspectives, while BLOOM provides an adaptable framework for delivering scientific education to different audiences. The following sections describe how these two frameworks were developed and applied during NariViva.

VIOCES: A Human Practices Framework

For a biomedical synthetic biology project, technical design alone cannot capture all the considerations surrounding a potential application. During NariViva, we therefore developed VIOCES, a Human Practices framework designed to systematically collect, integrate, and translate perspectives from different stakeholders.

Stakeholder Synthesis

Rather than treating stakeholder engagement as a one-time consultation, VIOCES organizes different perspectives into a structured process.

The six sources of information are not intended to be followed as a rigid sequence. Instead, they form a stakeholder map that teams can adapt according to their project.

For example, a therapeutic project may place greater emphasis on medical professionals and patients, while an environmental biotechnology project may prioritize industry, local communities, and environmental organizations.

BLOOM: An Education and Outreach Framework

Our Education program was developed around a second framework: BLOOM.

BLOOM provides a framework for designing educational activities for different audiences and communication contexts. It consists of five dimensions:

Modification

Rather than treating education as a fixed activity for a single audience, BLOOM emphasizes adapting scientific communication to different levels of knowledge, communication channels, and learning contexts.

Through Modification, BLOOM allows educational activities to remain flexible and responsive rather than functioning as fixed, one-time materials.

Together, the five dimensions of BLOOM provide a framework for designing, delivering, and adapting synthetic biology education across different audiences and contexts.

04

Reusable Educational Activities

GFP: Understanding Fluorescent Proteins

As part of our Education program, we developed a complete GFP educational activity package, including a structured activity plan and presentation slides.

The activity introduces GFP through an accessible explanation of fluorescent proteins and connects the concept to synthetic biology and biological engineering.

The package includes the materials needed to conduct the activity, the presentation structure, and the activity flow. The prepared materials can be directly reused by future iGEM teams or modified for different audiences.

Rather than providing only a description of what we did, we provide the activity materials themselves so that another team can use them as a starting point without developing the entire lesson from scratch.

Understanding Cells

We also developed a complete “Understanding Cells” educational activity package, including an activity plan and presentation slides.

The activity introduces fundamental concepts about cells and helps learners establish a basic understanding of cellular structure and function before moving toward more advanced biological concepts.

Like the GFP activity, the package was designed as a reusable educational resource. Future teams can directly use the existing activity plan and PPT, or modify the content according to the age, scientific background, and learning objectives of their audience.

Reusability and Modification

These two activity packages demonstrate the practical implementation of the BLOOM framework.

They are not only records of activities conducted by our team, but also reusable educational resources that can be transferred to other iGEM teams, classrooms, outreach events, or science communication activities.

The reusable materials include:

  • activity plans;
  • presentation slides;
  • activity structure;
  • teaching content;
  • discussion points;
  • materials that can be adapted for different audiences.

The principle of Modification allows these resources to be used as starting points rather than fixed lesson plans. Future teams can directly implement the activities or modify their content, difficulty, format, and delivery according to their own educational objectives.

06

Contribution Beyond NariViva

Our contribution is therefore not limited to the final NariViva design.

We contribute a set of biological parts, experimental knowledge, troubleshooting experience, and reusable methodologies.

Most importantly, we hope to demonstrate a reproducible workflow in which:

Parts are characterized, unexpected results are documented, protocols are shared, stakeholder feedback informs project development, and educational activities are adapted for different audiences.

Through these resources, future iGEM teams can build on what we learned rather than starting from zero.
